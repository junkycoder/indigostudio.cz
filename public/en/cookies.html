Accessibility and web analytics

Cookie banners and when you don't actually need one

That annoying banner is not mandatory. The only obligation is to collect consent to tracking — and you need it when you store more than the technically necessary minimum on a visitor's device.

A nuisance nobody wants

Cookie banners annoy everyone, and most of all the very people accessibility rules exist for. People click “Accept all” blindly just to make the window go away — which makes the consent meaningless.

For screen readers and keyboard navigation, the banner is often a trap: it captures focus, covers the content and can only be closed with a mouse. On top of that, the “Reject” button is often light gray — exactly what an automated accessibility audit flags as a contrast error.

We see it again and again: on an otherwise well-built municipal website, the only real accessibility problem is the cookie banner itself.

Why almost everyone has one

You may store or read information on a visitor's device (cookies, but also localStorage) only with their consent — in the Czech Republic under Section 89(3) of Act No. 127/2005 Coll. (zákon o elektronických komunikacích, the Czech Electronic Communications Act), and since 2022 as prior (opt-in) consent. The banner is just a way to collect that consent.

But you only need consent for what is not necessary. A banner is most often forced by cookie-based analytics (classic Google Analytics), advertising and marketing scripts, and embedded third-party content that tracks visitors.

Technically necessary cookies, on the other hand — login, security, language, shopping cart — don't require consent. You don't need a banner just for them.

You can do without cookies — and so without a banner

  1. Only necessary cookies

    Without tracking, a short note in your privacy policy is enough. No pop-up.

  2. Cookieless analytics

    Tools such as Plausible, Fathom or Matomo in cookieless mode store nothing on the device and collect no personal data. You can typically measure traffic even without consent.

  3. Server-side measurement

    You can get aggregate statistics from server logs, with no script in the browser at all.

  4. Click-to-load embeds

    Load a map or video only after a click. Until then, they don't run and don't track anything.

The result: less legal burden, a faster website and, above all, the most problematic accessibility element on the page is gone.

Before you turn off the banner

This is not legal advice. Every website needs to be assessed based on exactly what it loads. Take this as an invitation to stop treating the cookie banner as a necessary evil.
